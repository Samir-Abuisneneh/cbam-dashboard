## 1. Open decisions, ranked

### 1.1 Switch the EU CBAM formula to the benchmark mechanism

**Status: open. Highest priority of the three, though smaller than first thought.**

The model computes:

```
chargeable = embedded x CBAM_factor
```

Regulation (EU) 2023/956 Article 31 adjusts the obligation to reflect the free
allocation an EU installation producing the same good would receive. Free
allocation under Article 10a of Directive 2003/87/EC is calculated against a
product benchmark, not against a share of the importer's own emissions. So the
correct form is:

```
chargeable = max(0, embedded - benchmark x (1 - CBAM_factor))
```

The two agree only in 2034, when free allocation reaches zero.

**Evidence.** Ramsook et al. (2025) report a 22% CBAM burden on Trinidad and
Tobago ammonia exports. Reproducing it:

| method | horizon average | burden |
|---|---|---|
| published | USD 115/t | 22.0% |
| benchmark form | USD 110.8/t | 20.7% |
| current model | USD 77.6/t | 14.5% |

**Effect on this study, using the real benchmarks** (Halifax to Hamburg, 2030,
medium price):

| pathway | current | benchmark form |
|---|---|---|
| grey hydrogen | EUR 280 | EUR 376 |
| green hydrogen | EUR 34 | EUR 0 |
| blue hydrogen | EUR 56 | EUR 0 |
| grey ammonia | EUR 61 | EUR 79 |
| green ammonia | EUR 17 | EUR 0 |

**Important correction.** An earlier note in this session said the switch could
reverse the headline finding. It does not. The green premium closes only 5%
further for hydrogen and 8% for ammonia, because the production cost gap
(EUR 2,993/t for hydrogen) is far larger than any CBAM difference. Green already
sits well below the hydrogen benchmark of 6.84, so it owes close to nothing
under either formula. The earlier claim used a placeholder benchmark of 2.5 and
was wrong.

Worth doing for correctness, and it strengthens the blue-beats-green result
because blue drops to zero too. It is not a threat to the conclusion.

Both formulas sit side by side in `cbam_model/validation/reference_case.py`,
with a test that fails if the main one is switched without anyone noticing.

**To act on this:** read the 2026 to 2030 benchmark values (see 2.2 below) and
decide whether to switch, then rerun everything.

Now held in code as `rc.EU_ETS_PRODUCT_BENCHMARK_TCO2E_PER_TONNE`.

**Still to do.** These are the 2021 to 2025 values. The Commission adopted
revised benchmarks for 2026 to 2030 on 29 June 2026, cutting free allocation by
more than 16% on average, with ammonia and hydrogen both among the sectors
revised. Those figures have not been read out. Background:
https://refindustry.com/news/legislation/eu-updates-ets-free-allocation-benchmarks-for-2026-2030/

**Do not confuse two different regulations:**

| | what it sets | regulation |
|---|---|---|
| default value | what an importer uses without verified data | IR 2025/2621 |
| benchmark | what an EU producer's free allocation is measured against | IR 2021/447 |

An earlier note in this project pointed at IR 2025/2621 for benchmarks. That was
wrong.

to the linkage.

## 6. Things known to still be missing

1. **2026 to 2030 EU ETS benchmarks.** See 2.2. Needed before switching the CBAM
   formula.
2. **Outcome of the 13 July 2026 summit.** See 2.3.
3. **Conversion and shipping cost.** No owner, no source. Both are invariant to

   means deciding whether production cost should vary by year the way carbon

   is already the baseline in every result. What does not exist is a fossil
   cargo comparison, which would need energy equivalence and end use
   assumptions. Judged out of scope before 16 August.
6. **UK CBAM carbon price relief is implemented but dormant.** China prices
   neither product, so it is zero everywhere today. It exists so the model can
   represent China's documented plan to extend its ETS into chemicals.
